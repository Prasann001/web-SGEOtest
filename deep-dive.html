In Depth

Beyond the demo model

SGEO's hospital-school-street demo is a scaled-down version of a decision problem every power grid faces. Here's a closer look at how the logic works, and where the same idea applies at a much bigger scale.

The Core Idea, Explained Further

Every power grid is a balancing act between supply (how much electricity is being generated) and demand (how much every connected load wants to draw). Most of the time, supply comfortably covers demand. But when it doesn't — a cloudy week for solar, a calm week for wind, unexpected equipment failure — something has to give.

Utility companies handle this today through load shedding: deliberately cutting power to some areas so that others — the ones that matter most — stay online. SGEO automates exactly this decision using a simple, transparent rule set instead of a human operator making judgment calls in a crisis.

The model reads a live power-availability index (a combined reading of solar and wind input), compares it against four thresholds, and decides — every 500 milliseconds — which zones stay powered. Hospital-critical infrastructure never loses power. Everything else is prioritized by how essential it is to daily life, not by who shouts loudest or who's connected first.

Where Else This Applies

The hospital/school/street-light model is a teaching example — but the underlying logic (rank loads by priority, shed the least critical first, restore in reverse order) shows up anywhere power is finite and not everything can run at once.

🏠

Homes With Solar Panels

A home battery system could keep the fridge and lights running during an outage while automatically dropping non-essential loads like the water heater or AC — exactly SGEO's logic, just at home scale.

🌾

Rural Microgrids

Villages running on standalone solar/wind microgrids (common in areas without reliable grid access) need the same prioritization — wells and clinics before decorative lighting.

🚨

Disaster Relief Zones

Temporary power setups after natural disasters have to make hard calls fast — communication equipment and medical tents over everything else. An automated priority system removes the guesswork under pressure.

🏢

Campuses & Office Parks

Large campuses on partial backup power during outages could keep server rooms and emergency lighting alive while shedding parking lot lighting or non-critical HVAC zones first.

🔌

EV Charging Stations

A charging hub with limited grid capacity could prioritize emergency vehicles or pre-booked slots over opportunistic top-ups when demand spikes.

🏭

Industrial Backup Power

Factories on generator backup during grid failure often need to keep safety systems and critical production lines running while idling less time-sensitive equipment — the same shed-by-priority approach.

Why Priority-Based, Not Equal Sharing

An alternative approach would be splitting available power equally across every zone. SGEO deliberately doesn't do this — equal sharing under scarcity often means everyone gets just enough to fail: a hospital running on reduced power is far more dangerous than a hospital at full power while streetlights stay dark. Prioritization accepts an uncomfortable trade-off on purpose, the same way real utilities do, rather than spreading the shortage thin enough to compromise what matters most.
